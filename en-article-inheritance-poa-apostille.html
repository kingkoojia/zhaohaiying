Overseas Heir’s POA for China Inheritance: Notarize → Apostille → Chinese Translation

Notarize · Apostille · Chinese translation

Inheritance Updated: September 17, 2026

When an overseas heir cannot appear in China, the usual stall is not the slogan “do I need an Apostille?” It is what the inheritance power of attorney actually authorizes, the order of foreign notarization and Apostille, whether the Chinese translation matches the source, and which notary, registry, or bank will take the file. This page covers that line only: inheritance POA → notarize abroad → Apostille → Chinese translation / consistency notarization → the receiving office. For Apostille rules in general, see the Apostille guide for China. For identity, death, kinship, and title papers, see the overseas inheritance documents checklist. Nothing below promises a result, a timeline, or a fee.

When an inheritance POA is needed

If the heir cannot or prefers not to appear in person at a mainland notary, property registry, or bank, the usual path is to sign a power of attorney where they live and have a China agent carry the file. Whether a POA is required, and what the agent may do, follows the receiving office’s rules at the time. Do not assume remote handling is always available.

Draft the authority to match the steps you actually intend, not a single line that says “handle inheritance notarization.” The same instrument is often later used to:

  • inquire into the decedent’s real estate, deposits, or other assets;
  • apply for inheritance notarization, or support litigation and registration if notarization is not available;
  • complete property registration, open or activate a bank account, and submit tax papers;
  • if a sale and remittance will follow, cover FX purchase and related tax proofs. For that later order, see after inheritance: aligning title, tax clearance, and FX remittance.

If the grant is too narrow, the heir usually has to re-sign abroad and run notarization and Apostille again, often measured in weeks. Align the clauses with the target notary, bank, or registry list before the overseas signing, not after.

Correct order: notarize abroad → Apostille → Chinese translation → consistency notarization → receiving office

A sequence that tends to produce fewer reworks is:

  1. Name the receiving office and the authority scope first. Identify the mainland notary, registry, or bank (or a Hong Kong probate track). Align Chinese and foreign-language drafts (English, German, or other) on the same names, dates, and grant clauses before notarization.
  2. Notarize in the issuing country. The inheritance POA is typically signed and notarized where the heir lives. In the same round, the receiving office may also want notarized passport copies, death or kinship papers, or a foreign court inheritance instrument. Some paths also ask for a foreign-counsel opinion on heirs, a will, or marital property; that opinion follows the same notarization and authentication chain.
  3. Obtain an Apostille from the issuing country’s competent authority. China has applied the Convention of 5 October 1961 Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents since 7 November 2023. China’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs states that a public document from another Contracting Party for use in mainland China generally needs only that country’s Apostille, not consular legalization by that country and a Chinese mission. See the MFA notice of 23 October 2023 (checked 17 September 2026). Whether a country is a party, and when the Convention entered into force for it, follows the HCCH status table. Documents from non-parties usually still need consular or other authentication.
  4. Send or carry originals to China. Mainland notaries and banks commonly ask to see the Apostille original. Do not assume a scan or copy will do. If the estate also includes Hong Kong assets, mainland China and Hong Kong are separate tracks; original counts and conversion steps should be prepared separately.
  5. Obtain a qualified Chinese translation and mainland notarial certification that the translation conforms to the original (译文与原文相符). An Apostille authenticates signatures and seals, not the translation. Receiving offices read Chinese. Name, date, or authority mismatches between versions commonly lead to rejection, not informal acceptance.
  6. File with the receiving office. Mainland inheritance notarization, registration, and banks are one track. Hong Kong probate is another. A mainland consistency notarization is not generally accepted for Hong Kong use.

Do not reverse the order: applying for an Apostille before notarization, translating after authentication so the Chinese no longer matches the certified text, or using a mainland consistency notarization in Hong Kong, are common reasons for a rework. The Convention removes a consular step for Contracting Parties; it does not replace a receiving office’s review of translation, authority, and identity consistency.

Common stalls at receiving offices

The stalls below are recurring procedural patterns in inheritance-POA files. They are not a case story, and they are not a promise that avoiding them means a one-pass filing.

1. Incomplete authority

A POA that only says “handle inheritance notarization” may be treated as too narrow when the agent later needs asset inquiry, title change, a bank account, remittance, or tax proofs. A supplement usually means signing again abroad and repeating notarization and Apostille, not editing a few words in China.

2. Translation mismatch

If Chinese, English, German, or other versions diverge on names, dates, or grant clauses, a mainland notary may refuse the consistency notarization, or ask for a separate POA just to obtain that certificate. Align language versions before the overseas notarization, not after the Apostille is issued.

3. Identity and name variants

A Chinese name, former name, passport romanization, and Apostille spelling that do not match domestic records often trigger a same-person / identity-consistency certificate, also notarized and apostilled. That is a parallel track to the main POA, not something the main POA finishes by itself.

4. Offices still asking for pre-Hague consular legalization

After China began applying the Convention, some clerks still request embassy or consulate legalization out of habit. The Convention text and the MFA notice are the reference points, but whether a given office will take an Apostille is still that office’s call. Confirm with the specific notary, bank, or registry before the overseas signing: for a Contracting Party document, will they accept an Apostille, or do they still want consular legalization?

5. Treating mainland China and Hong Kong as one track

One mainland inheritance notarial certificate generally does not cover Hong Kong assets. The Hong Kong side needs its own Apostille originals and a Hong Kong translation, notary, or probate path. A mainland “translation conforms to original” notarization does not usually replace Hong Kong requirements.

6. Too few Apostille originals

Mainland notarization, a mainland bank, and Hong Kong probate may each want an original at the same time. Where tracks run in parallel, practice notes point to several original sets (a common working range is three to five), rather than one original circulating among offices. The count still follows each receiving office.

7. A short term or no sub-delegation

Notary, registry, and bank review can run for weeks or months. A short fixed term, or a grant that is silent on sub-delegation (转委托), can stall when the counter or the handling person changes. Write term and sub-delegation for the full intended chain, and ask whether that office accepts sub-delegation.

8. Jurisdiction of the receiving notary was not checked first

Movable and immovable property follow different rules. Cities also differ in whether a notary will take the file, what the checklist is, and how fees are charged. Switching mainland notaries after the overseas notarization can surface extra documents that were never authenticated. Lock the target notary and use its list before deciding what to notarize abroad.

Two further gaps appear often: if an heir lacks full capacity, foreign court guardianship or agency papers also need the Apostille and translation chain; some receiving paths ask for a foreign-counsel opinion on heirs, a will, or marital property, and that opinion is authenticated with the rest of the pack, not only the POA.

Checklist before signing abroad

Use this as a pre-signing check, not a nationwide list, and not a promise of a one-pass filing:

  • The intended receiving body is named (which notary or bank, or a Hong Kong probate path);
  • Authority clauses cover the full intended chain (inquiry, inheritance notarization or litigation, registration, bank, FX or tax, as needed);
  • Chinese and foreign-language versions match on names, dates, and grant clauses before notarization;
  • If a Chinese name, former name, or passport spelling differs, identity-consistency / same-person papers are ready and will be notarized and apostilled in the same round;
  • Each foreign public document needed in China will receive an Apostille (or other authentication if the issuing country is not a party);
  • Extra original Apostille sets are planned if mainland and Hong Kong, or several banks and notaries, will run in parallel;
  • Time is reserved in China for translation and consistency notarization;
  • Hong Kong assets, if any, have a separate translation and Hong Kong notary or probate path, not mixed with the mainland consistency notarization;
  • The receiving office has confirmed Apostille versus consular legalization for Contracting Party documents;
  • Term and sub-delegation are long enough for later review, not only for the filing day.

FAQ

If I have an Apostille, will a China office still ask for consular legalization?

For a public document issued in a Contracting Party and used in mainland China, the MFA notice says an Apostille from that country is generally enough. Whether a particular notary or bank still asks for consular legalization out of habit is that office’s requirement at the time. Non-party documents usually still need consular or other authentication. Party status follows the HCCH status table; this page does not freeze a country’s status on a given date.

Is “handle inheritance notarization” enough for the bank and the registry later?

Not necessarily. Asset inquiry, registration, account opening, or FX purchase may be treated as outside the grant. Write the authority for the full intended chain before signing abroad, so you are not running a second overseas round after the notary step.

Can a mainland consistency notarization be used for Hong Kong probate?

Generally no. Mainland China and Hong Kong are separate tracks. A mainland inheritance notarial certificate typically does not cover Hong Kong assets. Hong Kong needs its own originals, translation, and notary or probate arrangement.

How many Apostille originals should I obtain?

There is no nationwide number. If a mainland notary, a bank, and Hong Kong probate run in parallel, each may want an original. A common working range for dual tracks is several sets (for example three to five). Follow each receiving office.

If the passport spelling differs from the household name, is the apostilled POA enough?

Usually not. Mismatches among a Chinese name, former name, passport romanization, and the Apostille often require a same-person certificate, also notarized and apostilled. Treat it as a parallel item in the first overseas round.
