Cross-Border Wills in China: A Guide for Overseas Chinese with Chinese Assets

Estate Planning Updated: July 6, 2026

Summary: For overseas Chinese who own property, bank accounts, or equity in China, a valid will is essential to ensure their wishes are honored. This guide covers which will types are recognized under Chinese law, whether foreign wills are valid in China, the notarization process, and common mistakes.

Why Overseas Chinese Need a Will for Their Chinese Assets

Many overseas Chinese and foreign nationals of Chinese descent own property, bank accounts, or equity in China but have not made a will that is valid under Chinese law. Without a valid will, the distribution of these assets follows China's intestate succession rules under the Civil Code, which may not align with your wishes. A cross-border will ensures that your Chinese assets are distributed according to your intentions, avoids prolonged family disputes, and simplifies the inheritance process for your heirs.

Types of Wills Recognized Under Chinese Law

Under the Inheritance Book of the Civil Code of the People's Republic of China, seven types of wills are legally recognized, each with strict formal requirements:

  • Holographic Will (自书遗嘱): Written entirely by the testator in their own handwriting, signed, and dated. It cannot be typed or written by another person.
  • Attested Will (代书遗嘱): Written by another person on behalf of the testator, requiring two or more witnesses throughout the process. The writer, witnesses, and testator must all sign and date.
  • Printed Will (打印遗嘱): A will created by printing. The testator and two witnesses must sign and date on every page.
  • Audio-Video Will (录音录像遗嘱): Recorded through audio or video, requiring two witnesses present who must state their names and the date.
  • Oral Will (口头遗嘱): Only valid in emergency situations with two witnesses present. Once the emergency ends, it must be converted to written or audio-video form or it becomes void.
  • Notarized Will (公证遗嘱): Made through a notary public, carrying the highest evidentiary weight. When multiple wills conflict, the most recent one prevails, but revocation of a notarized will also requires notarization.

Key Update: Since November 7, 2023, the Hague Apostille Convention has been in effect in China. Public documents notarized abroad for use in China now only need an Apostille certificate instead of consular legalization, significantly simplifying cross-border document authentication.

Are Wills Made Abroad Valid in China?

This is one of the most common questions from overseas Chinese. Under Article 33 of the Law of the People's Republic of China on the Application of Laws to Foreign-Related Civil Relations, the validity of a will is governed by the law of the testator's habitual residence at the time of making the will or at death, the law of the testator's nationality, or the law of the place where the will was made. Specifically:

  • A will made abroad in compliance with local law may be recognized in China, provided its content does not violate mandatory provisions of Chinese law, such as the compulsory share rule.
  • However, for real estate located in China, Chinese law has stronger jurisdiction over both the form and substance of the will.
  • In practice, Chinese courts and notary offices scrutinize foreign wills carefully. To minimize risk, it is advisable to make a separate will for Chinese assets under Chinese law, or to have a Chinese attorney review the foreign will for compliance.

Why You Should Consider a Notarized Will in China

For overseas Chinese with assets in China, a notarized will offers several significant advantages:

  • Highest Evidentiary Weight: A notarized will has been reviewed by a notary office, ensuring its legality and making the inheritance process smoother.
  • Simplified Execution: Heirs can directly use the notarized will and a certificate of inheritance rights to transfer property title or withdraw bank deposits without going through court proceedings.
  • Reduced Disputes: Professional review by the notary substantially reduces the risk of formal defects or content challenges, minimizing disputes among heirs.

If you cannot return to China in person to arrange a notarized will, you can:

  • Obtain a notarized power of attorney from a Chinese embassy or consulate abroad, authorizing a trusted relative or attorney in China to handle the notarized will process on your behalf.
  • Arrange the process during a visit to China. For complex assets, consult with a lawyer in advance to prepare the necessary property documents and identification.

Key Content Considerations for a Cross-Border Will

1. Clearly Identify Your Assets and Their Location

List all assets located in China (real estate, bank accounts, stocks, equity, vehicles) along with their registration numbers. For cross-border assets, consider making separate wills in each jurisdiction to avoid legal conflicts of law.

2. Appoint a Suitable Executor

Under Chinese law, the executor manages and distributes the estate. Choose a trustworthy person residing in China as your executor to facilitate practical execution.

3. Respect the Compulsory Share Rule

Under the Civil Code, a will must reserve a necessary portion of the estate for heirs who lack the ability to work and have no source of income. This mandatory rule cannot be ignored; otherwise, the affected portion of the will may be invalid.

4. Clearly Designate Your Beneficiaries

State each beneficiary's full name, identification details, and relationship to you. It is also advisable to name contingent beneficiaries in case a primary beneficiary predeceases you.

Common Mistakes and Risk Warnings

  • Mistake 1: A foreign will is automatically valid in China. In reality, a foreign-made will requires a complex recognition process. It is strongly recommended to also make a will in China for Chinese assets.
  • Mistake 2: A printed will only needs my signature. The Civil Code clearly requires the testator and two witnesses to sign and date every page of a printed will. Failure to do so is a common cause of invalidity.
  • Mistake 3: A will can dispose of jointly owned marital property. A will can only dispose of the testator's own legal property. The spouse's share of jointly owned property cannot be disposed of by will.
  • Mistake 4: A notarized will cannot be changed. Notarized wills can be amended or revoked by making a new notarized will. The most recent notarized will prevails.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Can overseas Chinese make a will in China without a Chinese ID card?

Yes. Overseas Chinese can use their passport as a valid identification document when making a notarized will. It is advisable to confirm the required documents with the local notary office in advance; these typically include the original passport, property certificates, and marriage status documents.

Does China recognize wills drafted by foreign lawyers?

Wills made abroad in accordance with local law and properly notarized or apostilled may be recognized by Chinese courts on a case-by-case basis. However, to be absolutely certain, it is recommended to have the portion concerning Chinese assets drafted or reviewed by a Chinese attorney who specializes in cross-border inheritance matters.

What happens to a will after divorce or remarriage?

Under the Civil Code, a change in marital status (divorce or remarriage) after making a will may affect the validity of provisions concerning the former or new spouse. It is important to review and update your will whenever your marital status changes significantly.

How much does a notarized will cost in China?

Notary fees vary by region and the value of assets involved, typically ranging from a few hundred to a few thousand RMB. Compared to the cost of potential inheritance disputes, a notarized will is a highly cost-effective legal tool.
