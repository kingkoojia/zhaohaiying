Can a U.S. Citizen Inherit Real Estate in China? Full Process Guide

Cross-Border Inheritance Updated: August 2026

Chen, a U.S. passport holder, has a father who passed away leaving an apartment in Shanghai. From New York, she worries about whether she can inherit, how to authenticate the death certificate and kinship documents, and whether the sale proceeds can lawfully reach the United States. A U.S. citizen does not need Chinese citizenship or a long stay in China to inherit, but the document requirements and procedure follow a fixed path.

1. Yes, a U.S. citizen can inherit real estate in China

Chinese law does not remove inheritance rights based on nationality. Under Article 1122 of the Civil Code of the People's Republic of China, estate consists of the decedent's lawful personal property at death. Article 1123 provides that statutory succession applies unless a will or legacy-support agreement exists. Article 1127 sets out the statutory order: the first order is spouse, children, and parents; the second order is siblings, grandparents, and maternal grandparents. A U.S. heir follows the same rules as heirs in China.

2. Which law applies? Real estate follows Chinese law

Inheritance of real estate located in China is usually handled under Chinese law. Under Article 31 of the Law of the People's Republic of China on Application of Laws to Foreign-Related Civil Relations, statutory succession follows the law of the decedent's habitual residence at death, except that succession to immovable property follows the law of the place where the property is located. The law of the situs therefore governs the order of heirs, shares, and procedure. If a will exists, its validity falls under Article 33, which refers to the law of the decedent's habitual residence or nationality at the time of making the will or death, so each case needs individual analysis.

Sources: National Database of Laws and Regulations: Civil Code of the PRC; National Database of Laws and Regulations: Law on Application of Laws to Foreign-Related Civil Relations.

3. Step 1: Notarize U.S. documents and add an Apostille

U.S. death certificates, kinship certificates, and similar documents are usually notarized by a U.S. notary first, then authenticated with an Apostille by the Secretary of State of the relevant state. Since November 7, 2023, China applies the Hague Apostille Convention, so eligible U.S. public documents with an Apostille can be used in China without consular legalization. A Chinese translation should match the original; the accepting notary office and registration authority set the detailed format requirements.

4. Step 2: Obtain inheritance notarization or confirm rights through litigation

In uncontested cases, heirs normally apply to the notary office at the decedent's last habitual residence or where the main estate is located for an inheritance certificate. An heir in the United States can sign a power of attorney with an Apostille and authorize a lawyer or relative in China to act. The notary office reviews the will, family relationships, and the scope of the estate, and may ask for U.S. court documents or a same-person declaration. If heirs dispute shares or will validity, a succession lawsuit must be filed with the competent people's court.

5. Step 3: Title transfer and taxes

With the inheritance certificate or a final judgment, identity documents, and the original property ownership certificate, the heir applies to the real estate registration authority for transfer. Statutory heirs are exempt from deed tax when taking title through inheritance under Article 6 of the Deed Tax Law of the People's Republic of China. China currently has no inheritance tax. Registration fees and stamp duty may apply, and the local registration and tax authorities set the final requirements. Policy information verified as of 2026-08-31.

6. Step 4: Selling the inherited property and remitting proceeds lawfully

After inheritance, selling the property may trigger individual income tax, value-added tax, and other taxes as assessed by the tax authority. The proceeds are lawful property disposal income. With title transfer records, tax clearance documents, and other materials, the seller can apply for foreign exchange declaration, currency purchase, and cross-border remittance at a bank. Foreign exchange limits and document requirements depend on current rules. See also how overseas heirs transfer inherited deposits and stocks out of China.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Does a U.S. citizen need Chinese citizenship to inherit real estate in China?

A: No. Chinese law does not exclude heirs based on nationality. A U.S. citizen can inherit Chinese real estate under Chinese succession rules.

Q: Must a U.S. citizen return to China to inherit property?

A: Not necessarily. In uncontested cases, the heir can sign a power of attorney with an Apostille and authorize a lawyer or relative in China to handle inheritance notarization and title transfer.

Q: Do U.S. death certificates and kinship documents still need consular legalization?

A: No. Since November 7, 2023, China applies the Apostille Convention. U.S. public documents need notarization, an Apostille from the Secretary of State, and translation before use in China.

Q: Can a U.S. citizen remit the proceeds after selling inherited Chinese property?

A: Yes, through lawful channels. After title transfer and tax filing, the seller can apply for foreign exchange purchase and cross-border remittance at a bank, subject to current foreign exchange rules.
