Complete Guide to Cross-Border Inheritance in China

Inheritance Published: June 20, 2026 · Updated: October 15, 2026

Short answer: an overseas heir taking Chinese assets goes through two possible routes. Where the heirs broadly agree and the kinship evidence is in order, the route is a notarial certificate of inheritance rights followed by registration and the bank. Where heirs disagree or the notary cannot be satisfied, the matter goes to the people's court and registration follows the judgment. Foreign nationality does not restrict inheritance rights; it changes the paperwork, because documents issued abroad have to be authenticated. This page sets out who can inherit under Articles 1122 to 1127 of the Civil Code of the PRC, how the two routes differ, the order of steps, and what usually stalls a file.

Overview of Cross-Border Inheritance in China

When a person passes away leaving assets in China, heirs must navigate China's legal framework. This guide covers key steps, required documents, and common challenges.

Who Can Inherit?

Succession in China follows the Civil Code of the PRC. Article 1122 defines the estate as the lawful personal property left by a natural person at death, so the first practical question is always what actually falls inside the estate. Article 1123 then sets the order of priority: where there is a legacy-support agreement it applies; otherwise a will governs; without either, the estate passes under statutory succession. Article 1127 fixes the statutory order: first, spouse, children and parents; second, siblings, grandparents and maternal grandparents. Where a first-order heir exists, the second order does not inherit. "Children" includes children born in wedlock, children born outside it, adopted children and stepchildren who had a supporting relationship with the deceased.

Nationality does not change any of this. A foreign national or an overseas Chinese heir stands in the same position as a domestic heir, and the same order decides who is entitled. What nationality does change is the paperwork: identity documents issued abroad, death certificates and kinship evidence all have to be authenticated before a mainland notary or registry accepts them. Article 31 of the Law of the PRC on the Application of Laws to Foreign-Related Civil Relations decides the governing law: statutory succession follows the law of the deceased's habitual residence at death, except that succession to immovable property follows the law of the place where the property sits. For a flat in Shanghai, that generally means mainland law governs, whichever countries the heirs live in.

One point is worth stating plainly, because it decides many files: silence is not a waiver. Under Article 1124 of the Civil Code, an heir who wants to give up a share must do so in writing before the estate is distributed; an heir who says nothing is treated as having accepted.

Two Pathways

The notarial path. Where the heirs broadly agree, the family relationship is documented and the title papers are in order, the usual route is to apply for a notarial certificate of inheritance rights at a notary office, then take that certificate to the real-estate registry and to the banks. This is the shorter route in most files, and it is the one remote heirs usually aim for.

The litigation path. Where heirs disagree, one heir cannot be found or will not cooperate, the kinship evidence cannot satisfy the notary, or a will's validity is contested, the file goes to the people's court instead. The court decides the shares, and registration then proceeds on the effective judgment or court-mediated settlement. Litigation is not a failure of planning; for some families it is the only route that produces a document the registry will act on.

Which route applies is decided by the notary or the court on the papers, not by preference. A file that looks straightforward can move to litigation once a sibling refuses to sign, and a file that looks contested can settle into the notarial route if the heirs agree on shares before filing.

Key Steps

In practice the work runs in this order, and the order matters more than speed:

  1. Establish what is in the estate. Property title certificates, bank records, share or fund holdings, and any registered vehicle. This list drives everything else.
  2. Collect and authenticate the documents. Identity documents, the death certificate, kinship evidence and — where the heir acts remotely — an inheritance-specific power of attorney, notarised abroad and apostilled, with a Chinese translation. The document checklist and the authentication chain are set out in the overseas inheritance documents checklist and the POA and Apostille guide.
  3. Obtain the notarial inheritance certificate, or a judgment. This is the instrument that proves who owns what.
  4. Register the property. Title has to be in the heir's name before it can be sold; disposal before registration is not an ordinary resale and usually stalls. Civil Code Articles 230 and 232 are the reason: ownership acquired by inheritance takes effect when the succession opens, but disposing of the immovable property requires registration first.
  5. Deal with deposits, shares and funds at the bank. Banks apply their own intake list and often want originals, so the number of authenticated sets matters.
  6. If the property is to be sold, handle tax and remittance separately. Tax on a later sale is assessed by the tax authority, and the proceeds leave China through the personal-property transfer procedure described in inheritance to remittance.

What usually stalls a file

  • Names that do not match. A Chinese name, a former name, passport romanisation and the spelling on the Apostille that disagree are the single most common reason a notary asks for more.
  • An authority clause that is too narrow. A power of attorney limited to "inheritance notarization" leaves the agent unable to register title, open or activate an account, or handle tax — and re-signing means going through the foreign notary again.
  • Missing original purchase papers. For a later sale, the original cost of the property has to be evidenced; without the old contract and receipts the tax authority may assess on a deemed basis.
  • One heir who will not cooperate. Where a sibling refuses to sign, the notarial route closes and the file moves to court.
  • Too few authenticated originals. Notary, registry and bank often each want an original; one set usually does not cover the file.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Can a foreign national inherit property in China?

A: Yes. Chinese law does not restrict inheritance rights by nationality. What nationality changes is the paperwork: identity, death and kinship documents issued abroad have to be authenticated before a mainland notary or registry accepts them.

Q: Do I have to obtain a notarial certificate of inheritance rights?

A: Not always. Where the heirs agree, the kinship evidence is clear and the title papers are in order, the notarial route is usual and registration follows on the certificate. Where heirs disagree or the papers cannot satisfy the notary, the matter goes to court and registration follows the effective judgment or settlement.

Q: Can I complete the inheritance without returning to China?

A: In most files, yes. An inheritance-specific power of attorney signed, notarised and apostilled in the country of residence lets an agent notarise the inheritance, register the title and deal with the bank. The authentication chain is what has to be finished before the agent can act.

Q: What documents are needed?

A: Identity documents, the death certificate, kinship evidence, the property title papers and, where the heir acts remotely, an inheritance-specific power of attorney. Documents issued abroad must be notarised and authenticated (Apostille, or consular legalisation for non-contracting states) before use in the mainland.

Q: Is there inheritance tax in China?

A: China does not levy an inheritance tax, and an inheritance is not taxed as income. A statutory heir who receives land or housing by inheritance is exempt from deed tax under Article 6(5) of the Deed Tax Law of the PRC. Costs at this stage are fees charged by the notary and the registry. Tax on a later sale is assessed by the tax authority at the time of sale.

Q: How long does it take?

A: It varies widely with the facts: whether the heirs agree, how complete the kinship evidence is, how long authentication abroad takes, and whether one notary, registry and bank are involved or several. This page does not promise a timeline or an outcome.
