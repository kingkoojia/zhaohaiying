Can a U.S. Resident Inherit Shanghai Property Remotely?

De-identified case noteUpdated: November 13, 2026

Short answer: A U.S.-based heir can often complete an uncontested Shanghai property inheritance without returning to China when identity and family relationships can be verified, the supporting documents are acceptable, and the notary and property registration authority accept an authenticated power of attorney. The exact procedure still depends on the facts and the current requirements of the receiving authorities.

1. What the published case information establishes

Attorney Zhao Haiying handled a case in which an heir who had lived in the United States for about 30 years inherited a Shanghai property left by his parents. The published, de-identified summary states that the matter involved remote authorization, inheritance notarization and registration of the property transfer. In that case, with the relevant documents and procedural conditions in place, the client completed the steps without returning to China at any stage.

This page repeats only facts already published in the website summary. It does not invent details about the family, a will, disputes, the specific notary office, fees, timing or other identifying information.

2. The legal starting point

A foreign nationality or overseas residence does not by itself remove inheritance rights. Under Article 1122 of the Civil Code of the People's Republic of China, an estate consists of the lawful personal property left by a natural person at death. Article 1123 provides for statutory succession, testamentary succession and testamentary gifts, and Article 1127 sets out the order of statutory heirs.

For immovable property in Shanghai, the law of the place where the property is located is generally relevant to statutory succession. Article 31 of the Law of the People's Republic of China on the Laws Applicable to Foreign-Related Civil Relations provides that statutory succession is generally governed by the deceased's habitual residence at death, while statutory succession of immovable property is governed by the law of the place where the property is located. Wills, marital property arrangements and other family facts may affect the analysis.

Sources: Civil Code of the People's Republic of China; Law on the Laws Applicable to Foreign-Related Civil Relations. Verified as of 2026-11-13.

3. The usual sequence

  1. Confirm the heirs and the property title. Verify the death, family relationships, marital history and property records. The heir group should not be determined only from a client's account.
  2. Choose notarization or litigation. An uncontested case with complete records may proceed through inheritance notarization. A disagreement, missing heir or unclear estate may require proceedings before the competent people's court.
  3. Prepare U.S. documents and translations. The requirements for a death certificate, identity records, family relationship evidence, notarization, Apostille and translated documents depend on the document and the Chinese receiving body.
  4. Execute a power of attorney. If the heir cannot travel, a China inheritance-specific power of attorney may authorize a China-qualified lawyer to make inquiries, apply for notarization, submit documents, complete registration and collect documents.
  5. Complete inheritance notarization or litigation. The notary reviews the heirs and estate in an uncontested case; the court determines disputed issues.
  6. Register the property transfer. The registration authority processes the transfer using the inheritance certificate or effective court document, identity records and the original title documents.

4. When remote handling is more suitable

Remote handling depends on verifiable family relationships, a workable document chain, cooperation among the heirs and acceptance by the relevant authorities. A clear family tree and complete property records make the notarial route more suitable.

If a will is disputed, an heir cannot be located, the property is occupied, title records conflict or foreign documents cannot be completed, the matter may require litigation or additional investigation. “No need to travel” does not mean there are no procedural conditions.

5. Common problems

  • Names and identity records do not match: Old passports, naturalisation records, birth certificates and Chinese records may need identity-consistency evidence.
  • Only the death certificate is available: The notary may also need records establishing the spouse, children, parents and prior deaths.
  • The power of attorney is too narrow: The authority should cover inquiries, notarization, filing, registration and collection of documents as required by the case.
  • Inheritance and sale are confused: Confirm inheritance and register title first. Sale, tax, banking and remittance are separate later steps.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can a person living in the United States inherit Shanghai property without travelling to China?

In an uncontested case where identity and family relationships can be verified and the receiving authorities accept an authenticated power of attorney, a China-qualified lawyer can often handle the inheritance notarization and property registration.

Q: What is usually the first step?

First verify the heirs, family relationships and property title. The case then normally follows inheritance notarization if uncontested, or litigation if there is a dispute.

Q: Does every U.S. document require an Apostille?

The requirement depends on the document, issuing authority and Chinese receiving body. Eligible U.S. public documents may use an Apostille; translation or additional evidence may also be required.

Q: Can the property be sold immediately after inheritance?

After the inheritance right is confirmed and title is transferred, the heir may generally deal with the property. Sale, tax and cross-border remittance remain separate steps under the applicable current rules.
