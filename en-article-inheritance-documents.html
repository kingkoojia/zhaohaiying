What documents do you need to inherit China property from abroad?

Checklist · Remote POA · Common stalls

Inheritance Updated: September 13, 2026

Short answer: overseas heirs inheriting property in China usually need five groups of documents: the heir's identity documents; the death certificate of the deceased; proof of the family relationship; evidence of title to the property; and a power of attorney signed abroad, which normally has to be notarised and apostilled. Requirements differ between localities, so use this as a direction and then confirm the exact list with the office that will receive the file. Succession to real estate in China is handled under Chinese law (Article 31 of the Law of the PRC on Application of Laws to Foreign-Related Civil Relations); what counts as the estate and who inherits are set by Articles 1122 and 1127 of the Civil Code of the PRC. This page sets out principles and directions only, and does not promise an outcome or a timeline.

Overseas Chinese and foreign heirs often get stuck on documents, remote handling, and rework—not on slogans. This is a principles-level checklist. Which path applies, and which papers a notary, court, or registry will accept, depends on the competent authorities and the facts. Nothing below promises a result or a timeline.

Who this checklist helps

  • Overseas Chinese or foreign nationals inheriting property in China;
  • Heirs who prefer remote authorization rather than returning in person;
  • Anyone who still needs to split a notarial path from a litigation path before gathering a full document set.

Three common pathways

Article 1123 of the Civil Code of the PRC provides that succession follows statutory rules unless there is a will or a legacy-support agreement. Article 1127 sets the order: the first order is spouse, children and parents; the second order is siblings, grandparents and maternal grandparents. Where a first-order heir exists, the second order does not inherit. That is why proof of the family relationship matters: it establishes which order and which people are actually in the picture.

  1. Notarial inheritance when heirs are broadly aligned and the papers look workable, then property registration;
  2. Direct application by the heirs to the real-estate registration authority with the relevant materials;
  3. Litigation when heirs disagree, or notarization is not available on the facts.

Do not assume notarization will succeed, or that it will be faster, before the file has been reviewed.

Indicative document list (not a national uniform list)

Article 1122 of the Civil Code defines the estate as the lawful personal property left by a natural person at death, and excludes property that cannot be inherited by law or by its nature. Confirming what falls inside the estate first avoids collecting documents for assets that are not inherited at all.

Requirements differ by locality and receiving office. The following is an indicative direction only—not a nationwide checklist, and not a promise that this set will pass on the first try:

  1. Heir identity documents (passport or other valid ID);
  2. Death certificate of the decedent. A death certificate or similar public document issued by a government authority can, in most contracting states, receive an Apostille directly. A document that is not issued by a government authority generally must be notarized first, and an Apostille is then obtained for the notarial certificate.
  3. Kinship evidence, plus a will if there is one;
  4. Property title or registration documents;
  5. Power of attorney for a China agent, typically with local notarization and an Apostille (or whatever other authentication the issuing country and the receiving office still require). Order, translation, and receiving-office stalls: overseas heir’s POA for China inheritance. If the paper needed is a waiver of inheritance: overseas heir’s waiver of inheritance. If the Apostille and translation are already done and a mainland office still refuses the pack: Apostille done, still rejected in China.

Apostille: China has applied the Apostille Convention since 7 November 2023. Whether a given public document can use an Apostille still depends on the issuing country and on what the receiving authority in China will accept. Documents from non-contracting states usually still need consular or other authentication.

Remote POA — typical order

  1. Sign the power of attorney abroad and complete notarization plus Apostille (or other required authentication);
  2. Send the originals to China;
  3. The agent then handles notarization, litigation, and/or registration as the path requires.

Video witnessing or remote verification, if available at all, is case-by-case. Do not assume every notary or court accepts the same remote method.

Five common stalls

  • Weak kinship proof or a name mismatch (different spellings, name changes, incomplete household records);
  • Incomplete foreign authentication (signature only, or notarization without Apostille/consular authentication);
  • Heirs disagree or someone cannot be reached;
  • Trying to sell before inheritance completes and title is in the heir’s name (Civil Code, Articles 230 and 232: ownership acquired by inheritance takes effect when the succession opens; disposing of the immovable property requires registration first);
  • Forgetting post-sale remittance. If you later sell and want to move funds abroad, see Asset Realization and Fund Transfers from China.

For a process overview, see the complete guide to cross-border inheritance in China.

Timing

The time required varies widely from case to case. Authentication delays, supplements, multiple heirs in different countries, and contested cases usually take longer. Nothing here promises a timeline.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Do I have to obtain an inheritance notarization to inherit property in China?

A: Not always. Where the heirs agree, the family relationship is clear and the file meets the notary office's requirements, the usual route is notarization of inheritance rights followed by registration with the notarial certificate. Where heirs disagree, one refuses to cooperate, or the file cannot satisfy the notary conditions, the matter goes to court and registration proceeds on the effective judgment or court settlement.

Q: What authentication does a foreign death certificate need?

A: Public documents issued by a government authority can generally be apostilled between contracting states. Documents issued by non-government bodies are usually notarised locally first and then apostilled. Where the issuing country is not a party to the Apostille Convention, consular legalisation generally still applies. Whether a specific document is accepted also depends on the receiving office's own requirements.

Q: What can be used to prove the family relationship?

A: Commonly a household register, birth certificate, marriage certificate, personnel file records or a notarised relationship certificate. If names or dates on foreign documents do not match the Chinese records, further explanation or evidence is usually required, and this is the most frequent reason for a re-filing request.

Q: Once the documents are ready, can the property be transferred straight away?

A: Not necessarily. It also depends on whether the heirs cooperate, whether there is a will and whether its validity is disputed, and whether title to the property is clear. Whether notarisation is available or litigation is needed is determined by the notary office or the court on review. This page sets out document directions, not an outcome.
